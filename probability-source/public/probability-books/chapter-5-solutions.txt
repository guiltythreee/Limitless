 Problems 5.23–5.41
 5.23
 (a) Let N be the number of sixes. N~Bin(1000,1/6), with mean µ=166.667 and variance 138.889, so σ=11.785. With
 continuity correction,
 P(150≤N≤200)≈Φ((200.5−166.667)/11.785)−Φ((149.5−166.667)/11.785)≈0.926.
 (b) Given exactly 200 sixes, the other 800 rolls are equally likely to be any of the five non-six faces. Thus the number of
 fives is Bin(800,1/5), mean 160, variance 128, σ=11.314. Hence P(M<150)=P(M≤149)≈Φ((149.5−160)/11.314)≈0.176.

 5.24
 For one chip, p=P(T<1.8×10■)=Φ((1.8−1.4)/0.3)=Φ(1.3333)≈0.9088. Let N be the number of such chips among 100.
 Then N~Bin(100,p), with µ≈90.88 and σ≈2.87. We want P(N≥20), which is essentially 1 (the normal approximation gives
 a z-score below −24). Therefore the probability is indistinguishable from 1 at ordinary precision.

 5.25
 Let U be the number unacceptable. U~Bin(150,0.05), so µ=7.5 and σ=√7.125≈2.669.
 P(U≤10)≈Φ((10.5−7.5)/2.669)=Φ(1.124)≈0.869.

 5.26
 The test declares “biased” when H≥525. Fair coin: H~Bin(1000,0.5), µ=500, σ=15.811. False-positive probability
 ≈P(H≥524.5)=1−Φ(1.549)≈0.0607. Biased coin: H~Bin(1000,0.55), µ=550, σ=15.732. False-negative probability
 ≈P(H≤524.5)=Φ(−1.621)≈0.0525. The second probability is the chance of wrongly calling the biased coin fair.

 5.27
 Under fairness, H~Bin(10000,0.5), µ=5000 and σ=50. The observed 5800 is 16 standard deviations above the mean. A
 continuity-corrected normal tail is effectively zero. Thus the data provide overwhelming evidence against a fair coin.

 5.28
 Let X be the number left-handed. X~Bin(200,0.12), µ=24 and σ=√21.12≈4.596.
 P(X≥20)≈1−Φ((19.5−24)/4.596)=Φ(0.979)≈0.836. Assumptions: each student’s handedness is an independent Bernoulli
 outcome with the same probability 0.12.

 5.29
 Let K be the number of up movements. Then K~Bin(1000,0.52). The final price ratio is u^K d^(1000−K). Taking logs, the
 requirement is K ln(1.012)+(1000−K)ln(0.990)≥ln(1.30), which gives K≥approximately 508.9, hence K≥509. Here µ=520,
 σ=√249.6≈15.799. Thus P(K≥509)≈1−Φ((508.5−520)/15.799)=Φ(0.728)≈0.767.

 5.30
 At reading x=5, the likelihoods are fW(5)=φ((5−4)/2)/2 and fB(5)=φ((5−6)/3)/3. Equal error probabilities for the two
 possible conclusions mean equal posterior probabilities of black and white at this reading: αfB(5)=(1−α)fW(5). Therefore
 α=fW(5)/(fW(5)+fB(5)). Numerically fW(5)≈0.1760 and fB(5)≈0.0807, giving α≈0.686. (Here σW=2 and σB=3.)

 5.31
 (a) For X~Uniform(0,A), E|X−a|=[a²+(A−a)²]/(2A), 0≤a≤A. Differentiating gives (2a−A)/A=0, so the minimum occurs at
 a=A/2.
 (b) For a continuous distribution, E|X−a| is minimized at a median. An exponential(rate λ) median m satisfies
 1−e^(−λm)=1/2, so m=(ln 2)/λ. Locate the station there.

 P(T≥10|T>9)=P(T>10|T>9)=e^(−λ(10−9))=e^(−1/2)≈0.6065.

 Theoretical Exercises 5.1–5.33
 5.1
 Normalize the density: 1=a∫■∞x²e^(−bx²)dx. Using ∫■∞x²e^(−bx²)dx=√π/(4b^(3/2)), we obtain a=4b^(3/2)/√π.

 5.2
 For any real-valued Y, the positive and negative parts satisfy E[Y]=E[Y■]−E[Y■]. The tail-integral identity gives
 E[Y■]=∫■∞P(Y>y)dy and E[Y■]=∫■∞P(Y<−y)dy. Subtracting yields the required formula.

 5.3
 Apply Exercise 5.2 to W=g(X): E[g(X)]=∫■∞P(g(X)>y)dy−∫■∞P(g(X)<−y)dy. Writing each probability as an integral against
 f and interchanging the order of integration gives ∫g(x)f(x)dx. This is the usual expectation-of-a-function identity.

 5.4
 The referenced Corollary 2.1 is not reproduced in the uploaded Chapter 5 pages. Its statement is needed to give a
 source-faithful proof; it is therefore left as a source-dependent exercise rather than guessing which corollary is intended.

 5.5
 For X≥0, E[X■]=∫■∞P(X■>t)dt. Set t=x■, so dt=nx■■¹dx and P(X■>t)=P(X>x). Hence E[X■]=∫■∞nx■■¹P(X>x)dx.

 5.6
 Let X~Uniform(0,1) and define E■={X>a} for each 0<a<1. Then P(E■)=1−a, not 1; instead define E■={X≠a}. Each has
 probability 1, but their intersection over all a∈(0,1) is empty because every X∈(0,1) equals one of the indexed a. Thus
 P(∩■E■)=0.

 5.7
 SD(aX+b)=√Var(aX+b)=√(a²Var(X))=√(a²σ²)=|a|σ.

 5.8
 Since 0≤X≤c, X²≤cX, so Var(X)=E[X²]−(E[X])²≤cµ−µ²=µ(c−µ), where µ=E[X]∈[0,c]. The quadratic µ(c−µ) is maximized at
 µ=c/2, with maximum c²/4.

 5.9
 The standard normal density is symmetric about zero, so Z and −Z have the same distribution. Therefore
 P(Z>x)=P(Z<−x). The two tails are disjoint, giving P(|Z|>x)=2P(Z>x). Also P(|Z|<x)=1−2P(Z>x)=2P(Z<x)−1.

 5.10
 For f(x)=(1/σ)φ((x−µ)/σ), f′′(x) is proportional to [((x−µ)²/σ²)−1]φ((x−µ)/σ). Since φ is positive, f′′=0 exactly when (x−µ)²=σ²,
 i.e. x=µ±σ.

 5.11
 (a) Since φ′(z)=−zφ(z), integration by parts gives E[g′(Z)]=∫g′φ=[gφ]■∞∞−∫gφ′=∫zgφ=E[Zg(Z)], assuming boundary terms
 vanish.
 (b) Put g(z)=z■: E[nZ■■¹]=E[Z■■¹].
 (c) E[Z■]=3E[Z²]=3.

 5.12
 For X~Exp(λ), P(X>x)=e^(−λx). Exercise 5.5 with n=2 gives E[X²]=∫■∞2xe^(−λx)dx=2/λ², by integration by parts.

 5.13
 (a) Uniform(a,b): F(m)=1/2 gives m=(a+b)/2.
 (b) Normal: symmetry gives median µ.

 5.15
 For Y=cX, c>0, P(Y>y)=P(X>y/c)=e^(−λy/c). This is exponential with rate λ/c.

 5.16
 For X~Uniform(0,a), S(t)=1−t/a for 0≤t<a and f(t)=1/a. Hazard h(t)=f(t)/S(t)=1/(a−t), for 0≤t<a.

 5.17
 For Y=aX, a>0, S■(t)=S■(t/a). Differentiating gives f■(t)=f■(t/a)/a. Thus h■(t)=f■/S■=(1/a)h■(t/a).

 5.18
 The gamma density is f(x)=λ■x^(α−1)e^(−λx)/Γ(α), x>0. Substitute u=λx: its integral becomes
 [1/Γ(α)]∫■∞u^(α−1)e^(−u)du=1 by the definition of Γ(α).

 5.19
 For X~Exp(λ), its density is λe^(−λx). Then E[X■]=λ∫■∞x■e^(−λx)dx. With u=λx this is
 λ/λ^(k+1)∫■∞u■e^(−u)du=Γ(k+1)/λ■=k!/λ■.

 5.20
 For a gamma(α,λ) variable, E[X]=α/λ and E[X²]=α(α+1)/λ². Hence Var(X)=E[X²]−E[X]²=[α(α+1)−α²]/λ²=α/λ².

 5.21
 Γ(1/2)=∫■∞e^(−x)x^(−1/2)dx. Put x=u², dx=2u du, to obtain 2∫■∞e^(−u²)du=√π, using the Gaussian integral.

 5.22
 For gamma(α,λ), h(t)=f(t)/S(t), where f(t)=λ■t^(α−1)e^(−λt)/Γ(α) and S(t)=Γ(α,λt)/Γ(α) (upper incomplete gamma). Thus
 h(t)=λ■t^(α−1)e^(−λt)/Γ(α,λt). The gamma hazard is increasing for α≥1 and decreasing for 0<α≤1; the monotonicity
 follows from the corresponding monotone likelihood/hazard property of the gamma family.

 5.23
 For Weibull(α,β) with survival S(x)=exp[−(x/α)^β], density is f(x)=(β/α)(x/α)^(β−1)exp[−(x/α)^β]. Therefore
 h(x)=f/S=(β/α)(x/α)^(β−1). It increases if β>1, is constant if β=1, and decreases if 0<β<1.

 5.24
 For Weibull with location ν=0, F(x)=1−exp[−(x/α)^β]. Thus log{−log[1−F(x)]}=βlog x−βlogα, a straight line against log x
 with slope β. At x=α, F(α)=1−e^(−1)≈0.632, so about 63.2% lie below α.

 5.25
 Let Y=((X−ν)/α)^β. For a Weibull(ν,α,β), P(Y>y)=P(X>ν+αy^(1/β))=exp(−y), y≥0. Thus Y~Exp(1). Conversely, if Y~Exp(1),
 X=ν+αY^(1/β) has the stated Weibull distribution.

 5.26
 For Beta(a,b), the beta integral gives E[X]=B(a+1,b)/B(a,b)=a/(a+b). Similarly E[X²]=a(a+1)/[(a+b)(a+b+1)]. Subtracting
 the square of the mean yields Var(X)=ab/[(a+b)²(a+b+1)].

 Set Y=(X−a)/(b−a). For a<X<b, 0<Y<1. A positive affine transformation of a uniform variable has constant density:
 fY(y)=(b−a)·[1/(b−a)]=1, 0<y<1. Hence Y~Uniform(0,1).

 5.28
 The beta density is proportional to x^(a−1)(1−x)^(b−1). Its log derivative is (a−1)/x−(b−1)/(1−x), which vanishes at
 x=(a−1)/(a+b−2). For a,b>1 this is the unique interior mode. If a,b≤1 with a+b<2, the density is endpoint-peaked
 (U-shaped when both are below 1; one endpoint mode in the boundary cases). If a=b=1, the density is constant and
 every point is a mode.

 5.29
 For continuous F, P(F(X)≤u)=P(X≤F■¹(u))=F(F■¹(u))=u for 0<u<1. Therefore F(X)~Uniform(0,1).

 5.30
 If Y=aX+b and a≠0, then x=(y−b)/a and |dx/dy|=1/|a|. Therefore fY(y)=(1/|a|)fX((y−b)/a). If a=0, Y is the constant b and
 has a point mass there.

 5.31
 If X~N(µ,σ²) and Y=e■, then y>0, x=ln y, and dx/dy=1/y. Hence fY(y)=[1/(yσ√(2π))]exp{−(ln y−µ)²/(2σ²)}, y>0; zero
 otherwise. This is the lognormal density.

 5.32
 (a) For large independent uniform integers, the heuristic probability that both are divisible by k is 1/k²; conditional on
 dividing by k, their quotients are relatively prime with probability Q■. Hence Q■=Q■/k².
 (b) Since the events D=k partition all outcomes, 1=ΣQ■=Q■Σ1/k². Using Σ1/k²=π²/6 gives Q■=6/π².
 (c) The probability of no common prime divisor is the product over primes p of (1−1/p²), which equals 1/Σ1/k²=6/π² by the
 Euler product.
