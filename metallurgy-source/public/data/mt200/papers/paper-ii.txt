SECTION A · DERIVATIONS (46 marks)

A1. [6] Derive the torsion formula τ/r = T/J = Gθ/L for a circular shaft. State the assumptions clearly, and say why they hold only for circular sections. Evaluate J for a solid shaft and a hollow shaft, and obtain τmax = 16T/(πd³) for the solid case.

A2. [5] Show that G = E / [2(1 + ν)]. (Hint: a state of pure shear is equivalent to tension and compression of equal magnitude on planes at 45°. Find the strain along the diagonal of a sheared square in two ways.)

A3. [5] For a bar under axial load P (area A), derive the normal stress and shear stress on a plane whose normal makes an angle φ with the axis. Show that the maximum shear stress is σ/2 at 45°. Then derive Schmid's law, τr = σ cosφ cosλ, and state the maximum Schmid factor and what it implies for slip in a single crystal.

A4. [6] Derive the energy absorbed in a Charpy test, E = mgR(cosβ − cosα), from the pendulum geometry, and obtain the striking velocity. Explain how friction and windage loss is corrected, what the capacity of the machine is, and why the absorbed energy should not exceed about 80% of it.

A5. [6] Fatigue. (i) Define stress amplitude σa, mean stress σm, stress range Δσ and stress ratio R in terms of σmax and σmin. (ii) Derive the Goodman relation σa/σe + σm/σUTS = 1 (and with a safety factor n), and express allowable σmax in terms of R. (iii) From two points on a straight-line log–log S–N curve, obtain the Basquin exponent b. (iv) State how Gerber and Soderberg lines differ from Goodman.

A6. [6] Creep. (i) Write the Arrhenius form of the steady-state creep rate. (ii) Show how activation energy Q follows from creep rates at two temperatures under the same stress. (iii) Using the Monkman–Grant observation (dε/dt)ss · tr = constant, derive the Larson–Miller parameter T(C + log tr), and explain how it is used and where it fails.

A7. [4] Derive the true fracture strain εf = ln[1/(1 − RA)] from percentage reduction of area, and state the assumption. Explain why %EL depends on gauge length while %RA does not (use Barba's law e = eu + β√A0/L0), and why %RA is the better measure of local ductility.

A8. [4] Write the generalised Hooke's law for a triaxial state. Derive the volumetric strain in terms of σx + σy + σz, then the bulk modulus K = E/[3(1 − 2ν)]. Why must ν ≤ 0.5? Connect this to the volume-constancy assumption of the true stress–strain relations.

A9. [4] For a unidirectional fibre composite (fibre volume fraction Vf), derive the modulus for loading (i) parallel to the fibres (isostrain), and (ii) transverse to them (isostress). Show the fraction of the load carried by the fibres in case (i).

SECTION B · HARD CONCEPTUAL AND THEORY QUESTIONS (32 marks)

B1. [4] Describe the five levels of structure (atomic, crystal, micro, macro, mega) and give one property governed at each level. Distinguish structure-sensitive from structure-insensitive properties, with examples, and show through two examples how the same composition can give very different properties.

B2. [4] Relate the bonding in metals, ceramics and polymers to their modulus, strength, ductility, density and thermal behaviour. Why is the stress–strain curve of rubber non-linear, and why does E of a polymer depend on time and temperature? What does a composite achieve that its constituents cannot?

B3. [4] Why does a tensile specimen have a reduced gauge section with fillets and shoulders? What do standards such as ASTM E8 fix, and why? How does the choice of gauge length change the %EL you report, and what other test variables must be controlled?

B4. [4] Explain the yield-point phenomenon in low-carbon steel in terms of solute atmospheres and Lüders bands. How is yield strength defined for a material without a sharp yield point? Distinguish proportional limit, elastic limit and yield strength.

B5. [4] Why is torsion testing used, and what advantages does it have over tension for studying large plastic strains? Which specimen types are used and why must they be circular? Explain, from the stress state, why ductile metals fail in torsion on a plane perpendicular to the axis while brittle materials fail on a 45° helical surface.

B6. [4] What does an impact test signify? Compare Charpy and Izod tests (support, notch position, striking point). Discuss the factors that shift the ductile-to-brittle transition temperature (DBTT) and the criteria used to define it. Why do FCC metals show no DBTT?

B7. [4] Describe the three stages of a fatigue failure and the fracture-surface features that identify each. How do stress raisers, surface finish, residual stress and corrosion affect fatigue life? Distinguish endurance limit from fatigue strength, and high-cycle from low-cycle fatigue.

B8. [4] Explain the mechanisms of creep (dislocation, diffusional and grain-boundary sliding). Why is creep significant above about 0.4 Tm? Explain the three stages of a creep curve, and describe how turbine-blade alloys are designed to resist creep.

SECTION C · NUMERICALS AND DATA ANALYSIS (22 marks)

C1. [8] A solid steel shaft (d = 30 mm, L = 1.0 m, G = 80 GPa) carries a torque of 400 N·m.
(a) Find the maximum shear stress and the angle of twist (in radians and degrees).
(b) The shaft is replaced by a hollow one with the same outer diameter and an inner diameter of 20 mm. Find the maximum shear stress and the angle of twist. Compare mass and torsional stiffness with the solid shaft.
(c) The steel has σy = 350 MPa. Using the von Mises relation τy = σy/√3, find the torque at which the outer fibre of the solid shaft first yields, and the fully plastic torque for an elastic–perfectly plastic material. What is the ratio of the two?

C2. [7] A Charpy pendulum has an effective mass m = 20 kg at R = 0.80 m from the pivot. It is released from α = 140° (from the downward vertical) and, after breaking a specimen with an 80 mm² ligament below the notch, it swings up to β = 100° on the other side.
(a) Find the energy absorbed, the machine capacity, and check the 80% rule.
(b) Find the striking velocity.
(c) Find the absorbed energy per unit ligament area (J/cm²).
(d) Tests on the same steel at different temperatures gave the data below. Determine the DBTT by (i) the midpoint of the upper and lower shelf energies, and (ii) the 27 J criterion. Explain why the two values differ.
Temperature (°C): −80, −60, −40, −20, 0, 20, 40
Energy (J): 5, 8, 18, 45, 85, 105, 108

C3. [7] (i) Fatigue. A steel (σUTS = 600 MPa, fully reversed endurance limit σe = 250 MPa) is loaded between σmax = 400 MPa and σmin = 100 MPa. Using the Goodman line, decide whether infinite life is expected and find the safety factor. Find the largest σmax allowed if R is kept at 0.25.
(ii) Creep. Under a constant stress, the steady creep rate is 1.0 × 10⁻⁸ s⁻¹ at 873 K and 1.5 × 10⁻⁷ s⁻¹ at 923 K. Find the activation energy and predict the rate at 973 K. Hence estimate the time to accumulate 1% steady-state creep strain at 973 K.

SECTION D · BONUS (+8, not counted in the 100)

D1. [4] A composite has 30% by volume of glass fibre (E = 70 GPa) in epoxy (E = 3 GPa). Find the upper-bound (isostrain) and lower-bound (isostress) moduli. What fraction of the load do the fibres carry in the isostrain case, and which bound applies to loading along the fibres and which to loading across them?

D2. [4] A 12.5 mm diameter round bar with a 50 mm gauge length reaches a maximum load of 52 kN and breaks at 41 kN. After fracture the gauge length is 62.5 mm and the neck diameter is 8.0 mm. Find %EL, %RA, the true fracture strain and the true fracture stress. Explain why %EL would be smaller if the same neck occurred within a gauge length of 62.5 mm on the same bar.
