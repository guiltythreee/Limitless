SECTION A · STATEMENT EVALUATION (20 marks)
For each set, study the statements and choose the most appropriate answer. Each set carries 2 marks. There is no negative marking; one wrong word makes a statement wrong.

A1. Elastic and plastic behaviour of metals.
(A) Young’s modulus is structure-insensitive: it changes very little with grain size or cold work.
(B) Yield strength is structure-sensitive: it changes strongly with grain size, solute content and dislocation density.
(C) Compliance is the reciprocal of Young’s modulus, so its SI unit is Pa⁻¹ (m²/N).
(D) Elastic deformation of a metal is produced by dislocation glide, and this is why it is fully recoverable.
Options: (1) A, B and C correct; (2) B and C only; (3) all; (4) A and D only.

A2. True and engineering stress and strain in a tension test.
(A) Up to onset of necking, true stress is larger than engineering stress.
(B) For the same elongation in tension, true strain is smaller than engineering strain.
(C) After necking, engineering stress falls, while true stress of a strain-hardening metal keeps rising.
(D) Conversion σT = σe(1 + e) remains valid right up to fracture.
Options: (1) A and B only; (2) A, B and C only; (3) A, C and D only; (4) all.

A3. Resilience and toughness.
(A) Modulus of resilience is σy²/2E, the area under the elastic part of the curve.
(B) Of two steels with the same yield strength, the one with lower modulus stores more elastic energy per unit volume.
(C) A stronger material always has higher toughness.
(D) Toughness and modulus of resilience both have dimensions of energy per unit volume, the same as stress.
Options: (1) A, B and D; (2) A and D only; (3) B and C only; (4) all.

A4. Compression testing.
(A) Barrelling of a compressed cylinder is caused by friction between platens and specimen ends.
(B) A long, slender specimen is preferred because it gives a more uniform stress state.
(C) A ductile metal shows an ultimate-strength peak and necking in compression, just as in tension.
(D) Fracture of a barrelled specimen starts on the bulged outer surface, where a tensile hoop component develops.
Options: (1) A and D only; (2) A, B and D; (3) B and C only; (4) A, C and D.

A5. Hardness as a concept.
(A) Hardness is a fundamental property of a material, like density or melting point.
(B) For metals, hardness is roughly proportional to flow stress, with H ≈ 3σ.
(C) Mohs scale ranks engineering metals accurately because they span all ten steps.
(D) A rebound test ranks hardness because a harder material dissipates less energy in plastic deformation.
Options: (1) A and C only; (2) B and D only; (3) A, B and D; (4) B, C and D.

A6. Indentation hardness tests.
(A) Brinell hardness is load divided by projected area of the impression.
(B) Vickers hardness is nearly independent of load because impressions are geometrically similar.
(C) In a Rockwell test, a smaller permanent depth gives a higher hardness number.
(D) Rockwell minor load is applied to seat the indenter and remove effects of surface roughness and machine slack.
Options: (1) B, C and D; (2) all; (3) A and B only; (4) D only.

A7. Strengthening of metals.
(A) Cold working raises strength and hardness while leaving Young’s modulus essentially unchanged.
(B) Grain refinement raises yield strength (Hall–Petch) and does not generally reduce toughness.
(C) Strength gained by cold working is retained after annealing above recrystallisation temperature.
(D) Solid-solution strengthening arises because solute atoms distort the lattice and hinder dislocation motion.
Options: (1) A and B only; (2) A, B and D; (3) B, C and D; (4) all.

A8. Ductile and brittle fracture of alloys.
(A) BCC steel that is ductile at room temperature may fracture in a brittle manner at low temperature.
(B) FCC metals such as copper and aluminium generally remain ductile at very low temperatures.
(C) Higher strain rate tends to promote brittle fracture.
(D) A sharp notch lowers stress-state triaxiality and therefore promotes ductile fracture.
Options: (1) A, B and C; (2) all; (3) A and C only; (4) B and D only.

A9. Units and conversions.
(A) 1 ksi is about 6.9 MPa.
(B) Hardness of 200 kgf/mm² corresponds to about 1960 MPa.
(C) Strain rate has SI units of s⁻¹.
(D) Hardness in kgf/mm² and hardness in MPa are numerically the same.
Options: (1) A, B and C; (2) A and C only; (3) all; (4) B and D only.

A10. Tension-test practice.
(A) Tensile specimens have enlarged shoulders so failure occurs in the gauge length rather than at grips.
(B) Percentage elongation is independent of gauge length used.
(C) 0.2% offset yield strength is found by drawing a line parallel to the elastic slope, starting at 0.2% strain.
(D) Percentage reduction of area depends strongly on gauge length.
Options: (1) A and C only; (2) A, B and C; (3) B and D only; (4) A, C and D.

SECTION C · EMPIRICAL RELATIONS FROM DATA (20 marks)

C1. [10] Tensile strength and hardness of 8 steel grades are given below, with an approximate hardness conversion table. Develop a simple empirical relation between tensile strength (MPa) and hardness (BHN), and use it to estimate strength of steel with HRC 33.

Steel data (No.: strength in ksi; hardness):
1: 58; HRB 68
2: 75; HRB 80
3: 91; HRB 90
4: 108; HRB 97
5: 126; HRC 25
6: 141; HRC 30
7: 178; HRC 38
8: 208; HRC 45

Approximate conversion table (BHN / HRB / BHN / HRC):
100 / 56 / 226 / 20
120 / 68 / 253 / 25
140 / 78 / 286 / 30
160 / 85 / 327 / 35
180 / 90 / 371 / 40
200 / 94 / 421 / 45
220 / 97 / 481 / 50
240 / 100 / 543 / 55
Conversion table is approximate for steels (Brinell 10 mm ball, 3000 kgf). Interpolate linearly between rows.
(a) Convert every hardness to BHN and every strength to MPa. [3]
(b) Compute UTS/BHN for each grade, plot UTS against BHN and draw best line through origin. [3]
(c) State empirical relation UTS (MPa) = k × BHN with your k, and estimate UTS of steel with HRC 33. [2]
(d) State hardness range over which you would trust relation, and one reason it cannot be extended to all metals. [2]

C2. [10] Aluminium alloys and tempers were tested for tensile strength and Vickers hardness.
HV (kgf/mm²): 30, 45, 60, 85, 100, 125, 150
UTS (MPa): 110, 165, 225, 315, 370, 460, 545
(a) Compute UTS/HV for each alloy and find best line through origin, UTS = k × HV. Also find best straight line with an intercept, and comment on whether intercept matters. [4]
(b) Convert HV to MPa (1 kgf/mm² = 9.81 MPa) and express relation as UTS = c × H (both in MPa). Compare 1/c with constraint factor of about 3. [3]
(c) Compare k with value found for steels. Give one physical reason why they differ. [2]
(d) Predict UTS of alloy with HV = 110, and say how confident you are. [1]

SECTION D · APPLIED STRESS–STRAIN PROBLEMS (40 marks)
Each problem gives a real situation and stress–strain curves. Turn the situation into strain, read stress, and decide. Show numbers, not just words.

D1. [8] Ring on a shaft. A ring with a bore of 50.00 mm is to be press-fitted onto a solid steel shaft of diameter 50.20 mm. The ring is a hard, brittle material. Tension curves for both parts are shown (the shaft curve continues beyond 1%). Find out whether ring or shaft would crack or fail during press-fitting.
(a) Find strain imposed on ring and shaft (ignore change in the other part). [2]
(b) Read curves, compare with where each curve ends, and state which part (if any) fails and why. [3]
(c) Instead the ring is heated so it just slips over shaft with 0.04 mm diametral clearance. Find temperature rise needed (αring = 11 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹). [2]
(d) Could shaft alone be cooled in liquid nitrogen (−196 °C, from 20 °C, αshaft = 12 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹) to achieve same clearance? [1]
