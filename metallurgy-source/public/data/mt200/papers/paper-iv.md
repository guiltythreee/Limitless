 How this paper is built
   • It follows the four question types of the midsem: Section A statement evaluation (as in Q1),
        Section B matching (as in Q2), Section C an empirical relation from data (as in Q3), and Section
        D applied problems in which you turn a real situation into a strain and read the stress off a curve
        (as in Q4).
   • Section D carries the most marks (40). In every D problem the working is the same: (1) turn the
      situation into a strain (or a stress), (2) read the curve, (3) compare with yield and fracture, (4)
      conclude with numbers.
   • Curves are drawn on grids so that you can read them. Accept readings within about ±3% of the
      values in the marking scheme.
   • Use SI units unless told otherwise. Take 1 ksi = 6.895 MPa, 1 kgf = 9.81 N, and E = 200 GPa for steel
      unless a curve says otherwise.
   • Suggested time: A 25 min, B 20 min, C 30 min, D 75 min.

## SECTION A · STATEMENT EVALUATION (20 marks)
 For each set, study the statements and choose the most appropriate answer. Each set carries 2 marks. There is no
 negative marking, but read every statement fully: one wrong word makes a statement wrong.

A1. Elastic and plastic behaviour of metals.
 (A) Young's modulus is structure-insensitive: it changes very little with grain size or cold work.
 (B) Yield strength is structure-sensitive: it changes strongly with grain size, solute content and
     dislocation density.
 (C) Compliance is the reciprocal of Young's modulus, so its SI unit is Pa−1 (m2/N).
 (D) Elastic deformation of a metal is produced by dislocation glide, and this is why it is fully
    recoverable.
    (1) Statements (A), (B) and (C) are correct
    (2) Statements (B) and (C) only are correct
    (3) All the statements are correct
    (4) Statements (A) and (D) only are correct

A2. True and engineering stress and strain in a tension test.
 (A) Up to the onset of necking, the true stress is larger than the engineering stress.
 (B) For the same elongation in tension, the true strain is smaller than the engineering strain.
 (C) After necking, the engineering stress falls, while the true stress of a strain-hardening metal keeps
     rising.
 (D) The conversion σT = σe(1 + e) remains valid right up to the fracture point.
    (1) (A) and (B) only are correct
    (2) (A), (B) and (C) only are correct
    (3) (A), (C) and (D) only are correct
    (4) All the statements are correct

A3. Resilience and toughness.
 (A) The modulus of resilience is σy2/2E, the area under the elastic part of the curve.
 (B) Of two steels with the same yield strength, the one with the lower modulus stores more elastic
     energy per unit volume.
 (C) A stronger material always has a higher toughness.
 (D) Toughness and the modulus of resilience both have the dimensions of energy per unit volume,
    which are the same as those of stress.
    (1) (A), (B) and (D) are correct
    (2) (A) and (D) only are correct
    (3) (B) and (C) only are correct
    (4) All the statements are correct

A4. Compression testing.
 (A) Barrelling of a compressed cylinder is caused by friction between the platens and the specimen
     ends.
 (B) A long, slender specimen is preferred because it gives a more uniform stress state.
 (C) A ductile metal shows an ultimate-strength peak and necking in compression, just as it does in
     tension.
 (D) Fracture of a barrelled specimen starts on the bulged outer surface, where a tensile hoop
    component develops.
    (1) (A) and (D) only are correct
    (2) (A), (B) and (D) are correct
    (3) (B) and (C) only are correct
    (4) (A), (C) and (D) are correct

A5. Hardness as a concept.
 (A) Hardness is a fundamental property of a material, like density or melting point.
 (B) For metals, hardness is roughly proportional to the flow stress, with H ≈ 3σ.
 (C) The Mohs scale ranks engineering metals accurately because they span all ten of its steps.
 (D) A rebound test ranks hardness because a harder material dissipates less energy in plastic
    deformation.
    (1) (A) and (C) only are correct
    (2) (B) and (D) only are correct
    (3) (A), (B) and (D) are correct
    (4) (B), (C) and (D) are correct

A6. Indentation hardness tests.
 (A) Brinell hardness is the load divided by the projected area of the impression.
 (B) Vickers hardness is nearly independent of the load because the impressions are geometrically
     similar.
 (C) In a Rockwell test, a smaller permanent depth gives a higher hardness number.
 (D) The Rockwell minor load is applied to seat the indenter and to remove the effect of surface
    roughness and machine slack.
    (1) (B), (C) and (D) are correct
    (2) All the statements are correct
    (3) (A) and (B) only are correct
    (4) Only (D) is correct

A7. Strengthening of metals.
 (A) Cold working raises the strength and hardness while leaving Young's modulus essentially
     unchanged.
 (B) Grain refinement raises the yield strength (Hall–Petch) and does not generally reduce toughness.
 (C) The strength gained by cold working is retained after annealing above the recrystallisation
     temperature.
 (D) Solid-solution strengthening arises because solute atoms distort the lattice and hinder dislocation
    motion.
    (1) (A) and (B) only are correct
    (2) (A), (B) and (D) are correct
    (3) (B), (C) and (D) are correct
    (4) All the statements are correct

A8. Ductile and brittle fracture of alloys.
 (A) A BCC steel that is ductile at room temperature may fracture in a brittle manner at low
     temperature.
 (B) FCC metals such as copper and aluminium generally remain ductile at very low temperatures.
 (C) A higher strain rate tends to promote brittle fracture.
 (D) A sharp notch lowers the triaxiality of the stress state and therefore promotes ductile fracture.
    (1) (A), (B) and (C) are correct
    (2) All the statements are correct
    (3) (A) and (C) only are correct
    (4) (B) and (D) only are correct

A9. Units and conversions.
 (A) 1 ksi is about 6.9 MPa.
 (B) A hardness of 200 kgf/mm2 corresponds to about 1960 MPa.
 (C) Strain rate has SI units of s−1.
 (D) Hardness in kgf/mm2 and hardness in MPa are numerically the same.
    (1) (A), (B) and (C) are correct
    (2) (A) and (C) only are correct
    (3) All the statements are correct
    (4) (B) and (D) only are correct
